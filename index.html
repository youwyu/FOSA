Robots are built for broad use. Which tasks can each one actually learn?

Semantic plausibility is not physical feasibility. Training tasks are usually handcrafted or sampled from large language models, chosen because they sound reasonable. Yet a task that looks simple can be physically hard for a given robot, while a non-obvious behavior can be easy to learn and useful for composing longer skills.

Our idea: let the robot's own learning decide. FOSA learns a Generative Flow Network that constructs symbolic task skeletons edit by edit. Each skeleton is compiled into a simulation environment and trained with PPO, and the outcome rewards the generator for tasks on the robot's empirical capability frontier: learnable under the training budget, but not yet mastered.

Discovery concentrates where the frontier lies. Across a wheeled vehicle, a dexterous hand, a quadruped and a humanoid, FOSA reaches the highest frontier proximity on the vehicle (0.30 vs. 0.23), ties the best method on the humanoid, and gives frontier-bearing task families 1.30–1.78× the share of an even split on three of the four robots. Whether this also improves transfer to held-out tasks is still open: at 256 tasks, the distilled student does not yet favor it.

trajectory balance updates the abstract policy

𝓕(x) = 4J(1 − J) · max(0, J − J0)J

The first factor is the normalized variance of final success, largest when the policy succeeds half the time. The second is the share of that success acquired by training. 𝓕 is positive exactly on the frontier, J0 < J < 1, and has no target level or width to tune.

  1. Construct a skeleton

    A skeleton lists typed entities, an initial symbolic state, goal literals, operator schemas, continuous arguments and feasibility constraints. Invalid edits are masked, and stop is enabled only once the skeleton is complete.

  2. Ground it in learning

    The language model only compiles: it verbalizes the skeleton, organizes simulator assets and fills reward templates. The learned proposal distribution is the GFlowNet, so selection rests on PPO outcomes rather than on how plausible a task sounds.

  3. Search near the frontier

    Frontier skeletons cluster under the grammar's edits. Local search starts from an archived skeleton drawn in proportion to R, backtracks k ∈ {1, 2, 3} edits with PB and completes the skeleton with PF.

After discovery, the archive of task policies is distilled with DAgger into a single language-conditioned student (MiniVLA), which is evaluated on held-out tasks never seen during discovery.

Intuition is a poor guide to difficulty

  • Vehicle

    A short target-reaching task becomes surprisingly hard once the robot must also stop at the goal.

  • Hand

    Dynamic manipulation such as sphere reorientation and spinning is easier than basic reach, touch, grasp and hold.

  • Quadruped

    As for the vehicle, a short reaching task that ends with a controlled stop is harder than it looks.

  • Humanoid

    Static balance on rough ground or under payloads is learned more reliably than obstacle crossing and some carrying variants.

These easy–hard inversions are why FOSA grounds task selection in policy learning rather than in how plausible a task sounds.

At 256 tasks per robot, frontier-focused discovery and held-out transfer are not yet aligned: no curriculum statistic we measured, including frontier share and the number of task families covered, predicts student success with a consistent sign across robots. We regard this as the main open problem.

Real-World Transfer

We deploy distilled students on a ClearPath Jackal and a Unitree Go2, each running on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin with an Intel RealSense D435i at 30 Hz for policy inference and an OS1-64 LiDAR for 100 Hz LiDAR–inertial odometry. The benchmark follows the held-out tasks of the simulation evaluation.

These students were distilled from an earlier FOSA configuration, before local search and the shared reward. The main failure case is porch delivery, where low-friction contact, stair traversal and posture transitions amplify errors.
